Why we remember the Colored Women's Clubs

In 1896, Black women's clubs from across the country joined to form the National Association of Colored Women. Their motto was “Lifting as we climb.”

When schools, libraries and charities shut Black families out, the clubwomen built their own: kindergartens, reading rooms, libraries, orphanages and homes for the elderly. In 1912 they started a scholarship fund for young Black women going to college. By 1916 they had more than 300 clubs and nearly 100,000 members.

They paid for it the way we're doing it now: picnics, bazaars, raffles and concerts. When club president Mary B. Talbert asked every clubwoman and child to give toward saving Frederick Douglass's home, the mortgage was paid off in about two years. Small gifts, many hands. Their story is the one our book tells, and most children have never heard it.

Meet a few of the clubwomen

Mary Church Terrell, first president, later a founding member of the NAACP.

Ida B. Wells, whose campaign against lynching the clubs carried nationwide.

Harriet Tubman, who led others out of slavery and spoke at the 1896 founding meeting, where the women rose to cheer her.

Did you know?

In 1900, a national women's club convention in Milwaukee would not seat a Black club.

Josephine St. Pierre Ruffin came to the General Federation of Women's Clubs meeting as a delegate of her Boston club for Black women. When the leaders learned its members were Black, they told her she could sit only for the white clubs she also belonged to. She refused and demanded her club be recognized. Newspapers across the country covered the “Ruffin Incident.” Five years earlier, she had called the first national conference of Black women, which grew into the National Association of Colored Women.
